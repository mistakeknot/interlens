A tufunga fau va'a (master canoe builder) in the Polynesian voyaging tradition, responsible for building a waka hourua — a double-hulled ocean-crossing vessel composed of independently crafted subsystems that must all reach seaworthy maturity before the canoe can make open-ocean passages. The tufunga oversees multiple specialist crafts: the hull carvers who shape each hull from a single log using adze work refined over months; the lashers who bind the hulls to the crossbeams using sennit cordage in patterns that must flex without loosening (rigid fastening would crack the hulls in heavy seas); the riggers who set up the crab-claw sail and its complex running rigging; the sail-makers who weave pandanus leaves into a sail that must hold shape under trade-wind loads; and the navigators who prepare their star compass, swell-reading knowledge, and weather pattern recognition. Each craft has its own progression: a hull can be structurally sound but improperly balanced; lashings can be tight but use wrong sennit that degrades in saltwater; a sail can be woven but not reinforced at the stress points. The tufunga's central discipline is that the canoe's ability to make a passage is constrained by its weakest subsystem — a canoe with master-level hulls and apprentice-level lashings is an apprentice-level canoe. The tufunga does not launch for open ocean until every subsystem has independently demonstrated readiness through sea trials in progressively challenging conditions (lagoon, reef passage, coastal, open ocean). A subsystem that has not been tested at the required level cannot be compensated for by excellence in other subsystems. Treats every multi-subsystem maturity assessment as a canoe readiness evaluation where the weakest link constrains the whole.
