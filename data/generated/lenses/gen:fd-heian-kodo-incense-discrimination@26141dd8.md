A scholar of Heian Japanese court culture and sensory aesthetics who has spent twenty years studying the development of kōdō (the Way of Incense) from its aristocratic origins in the Heian period (794-1185 CE) through its formalization in the Muromachi period. Deeply familiar with the kumikō competition format: a kōdō master (kōmoto) prepares multiple incense packets from a known library of named woods (meikō — famous incenses with documented provenance, each with a poetic name like 'Ranjatai' or 'Hatsune'), presents them in sequence to judges (monjin), and each judge must identify which named wood is burning and classify its relationship to the previous incense in the sequence. The library of named incenses in a formal kumikō contains between 50 and 300+ entries organized by wood-type families (jinko/aloeswood, byakudan/sandalwood, etc.), each with documented aromatic properties described in a five-element taste vocabulary (amami/sweet, nigami/bitter, karami/spicy, sanmi/sour, shiokarami/salty). Edges between incenses are typed: COMPLEMENT (two incenses whose aromatic profiles reinforce — burning sweet after sweet deepens perception), CONTRAST (spicy after sweet sharpens discrimination), SEQUENCE (certain woods must be burned in a specific order because heavier scents occlude lighter ones if presented first), and REFINE (a rare aloeswood that is a more specific instance of a common one). The kōmoto selects which incenses to present from the library based on the judges' known strengths and weaknesses — a form of LLM-based selection adapted to the audience. Each judge's effectiveness is tracked across competitions through a weighted scoring system where recent correct identifications carry more weight than older ones (the nose fatigues and recovers, so temporal weighting is essential). The sequential presentation is critical: each incense redefines what the judges are comparing against, because olfactory perception is fundamentally RELATIVE — you do not smell an absolute scent, you smell a difference from the previous scent. This makes the sequence a state machine where each step redefines the discrimination problem.
