## Task Context

Garden Salon's governance model is per-garden with platform invariants. Attribution tracks contributions. The evolutionary path defers governance design. The review examines these decisions through the lens of the Dutch polder model — governance forged by the physical reality of shared flood risk.

## Review Approach

### 1. The shared risk principle
- In the waterschappen, everyone behind a dike shares the same flood risk regardless of wealth, status, or contribution to dike maintenance. This creates a non-negotiable alignment.
- What is the shared risk in a Garden Salon garden? If a merge corrupts state, all participants are affected equally. If governance is captured, all contributors lose. Is this shared risk made visible to participants?
- The waterschappen were successful because the shared risk was OBVIOUS (you can see the water). In Garden Salon, the shared risks (state corruption, governance capture, attribution gaming) are abstract. How do you make abstract risks visible?

### 2. Belang, betaling, zeggenschap — interest, payment, say
- The core polder principle: those who have interest in the outcome, who pay for the maintenance, and who have decision-making power should be the SAME people. When these three separate, governance fails.
- In Garden Salon: interest = participants using the garden. Payment = contributors providing compute (BYOK). Say = governance weight (determined by the garden's model).
- The brainstorm warns about capital capturing creative governance (patrons vs creative directors sharing "participant" role). This is EXACTLY the polder failure mode: when those who pay (compute) and those who have interest (creative work) are different people, the one who pays captures governance.
- Is the future "patron" role sufficient to separate these? Or does Garden Salon need the waterschappen's explicit "those who pay maintain but do not govern" principle?

### 3. Daily management vs emergency governance
- Waterschappen distinguish between dagelijks bestuur (daily board operations — maintenance, monitoring) and dijkgraaf authority (emergency powers during flood). Normal governance is deliberative. Emergency governance is authoritative.
- Garden Salon distinguishes normal operations (optimistic, post-hoc annotation) from governance transitions (immutable records, cooling periods). But what about mid-level emergencies? What if an agent is producing harmful output rapidly? Is there an emergency "close the floodgates" mechanism?
- The Owner can presumably remove an agent. But what if the Owner IS the problem? In waterschappen, the dijkgraaf can be overridden by the provincial executive. What overrides the Owner?

### 4. The obligation to contribute
- In polder governance, maintenance is not optional. Everyone behind the dike MUST contribute to maintenance proportional to their interest, or they lose their vote.
- In Garden Salon, participation is voluntary. But the governance brainstorm suggests attribution (contribution) should influence governance weight. What about free-riders — participants who benefit from the garden but contribute nothing? In cooperative gardens, should there be a minimum contribution threshold for governance participation?

### 5. The 800-year lesson
- The waterschappen have operated for 800 years. Their survival comes from three principles: (1) the shared risk must be real and visible, (2) governance must map to physical reality (water doesn't respect theory), (3) the system must outlive its founders.
- Garden Salon is building governance for a novel situation (human-agent collaboration). But the polder model suggests the FIRST question should be: what is the shared risk that aligns all participants? If the answer is unclear, the governance system will be unstable regardless of how well-designed it is.

## Severity Calibration

- **P0:** The interest/payment/say separation (patron vs creative director) is acknowledged but unresolved — this is the primary governance failure mode
- **P1:** No emergency governance mechanism for when normal operations fail
- **P2:** Shared risk is abstract and not surfaced to participants
- **P3:** Free-rider problem in cooperative gardens is unaddressed
