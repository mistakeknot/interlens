### 1. Call-and-response vs lecture transmission: the naqqsheh-khan calls a rhythmic

- Call-and-response vs lecture transmission: the naqqsheh-khan calls a rhythmic sequence ('khaki, lajevardi, sabz, do-dar...') and the weavers respond by knotting — the pattern knowledge lives in the call-and-response exchange, not in either party alone. Does the mentorship model allow for a teaching style where the aesthetic knowledge is encoded in the collaborative production event itself, rather than in demonstration followed by imitation? The current design's 'personality modulates XP rate' model assumes one-directional transfer.

### 2. The pattern-caller's independence from weaving skill: in Persian carpet

- The pattern-caller's independence from weaving skill: in Persian carpet workshops, the naqqsheh-khan was often a specialist who could not weave — their skill was purely in pattern knowledge and rhythmic transmission. Does the eligibility gate (mentor must be skill-9 in the relevant domain) correctly capture all teaching archetypes, or does it exclude the 'pattern specialist' who can transmit aesthetic knowledge without being a peak practitioner?

### 3. Pattern corruption through substitution: when a weaver had to substitute a

- Pattern corruption through substitution: when a weaver had to substitute a color due to dye shortage, the pattern-caller had to improvise a compatible sequence in real-time — improvisations sometimes created new sub-patterns that became canonical. Does the system model teaching moments where the mentor's aesthetic knowledge is tested by constraint (resource shortage, student's skill floor) and where improvisations under constraint become the most memorable transmitted aesthetic?

### 4. Workshop simultaneity: a carpet workshop had multiple weavers at the same loom

- Workshop simultaneity: a carpet workshop had multiple weavers at the same loom, all receiving instruction simultaneously from one pattern-caller. Does the one-apprentice-per-mentor cap correctly model the transmission dynamics, or should certain 'pattern-caller' teaching archetypes be able to transmit the same aesthetic to multiple students simultaneously, producing a school cohort rather than a chain?

### 5. The pattern lives in performance

- The pattern lives in performance, not in the carpet: the naqqsheh-khan's knowledge was lost when the caller died, even though thousands of carpets encoded the pattern — the carpets could be reproduced but only by someone who already knew the calling sequence. Does the 'In the style of [Master]' composition property suggest the style can be reconstructed from the artifacts, when in fact the generative knowledge (the calling sequence) may be irretrievably lost?

### 6. Seasonal workshop rhythms: carpet production organized around wet-season

- Seasonal workshop rhythms: carpet production organized around wet-season weaving and dry-season dyeing cycles — master-student interaction was intensive during weaving season and sparse during dye preparation. Does the apprenticeship model support rhythmic intensity patterns where the mentor-student bond is highly active during production events (revels, collaborative compositions) and dormant during preparation phases?

- **P2**: Eligibility gate excludes aesthetic specialists who cannot perform at peak

- **P3**: Style artifacts suggest style reconstructability that may not exist
  - When: Compositions carry 'In the style of [Master]' and 'Of the [School Name] School' properties — if all school members die, these labels remain on existing artifacts, implying the style could be reverse-engineered by a future student who studies the compositions; the naqqsheh-khan tradition suggests the generative knowledge (the calling sequence) is completely unrecoverable from the outputs alone
