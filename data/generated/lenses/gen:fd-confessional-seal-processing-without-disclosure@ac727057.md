## Task Context

Reviewing the encryption design brainstorm for Garden Salon, a multiplayer human-agent collaboration workspace. The brainstorm presents five design options ranging from encrypted-at-rest to full E2E, with open questions about AI proxy access, governance on encrypted content, and key succession. The core paradox: the system must process content collaboratively (real-time CRDT sync, AI analysis, governance hashing) while preventing the platform operator from reading it.

## Review Approach

### 1. The active

- The active-processor paradox: the AI proxy MUST read plaintext to send to LLMs (brainstorm Dimension 3). The confessional solves exactly this — the priest reads, processes, and responds to content under a binding operational constraint against disclosure. Is there an architectural equivalent of the Seal — a provable constraint that the server processes content but cannot exfiltrate or persist it? This is distinct from E2E encryption (where the server never sees content) and from trust (where you hope the server behaves). The Seal is an ENFORCEABLE OPERATIONAL CONSTRAINT on an active processor.

### 2. The forum internum / forum externum boundary: canon law d...

- The forum internum / forum externum boundary: canon law distinguishes sharply between what the priest knows 'in the internal forum' (confession) and the 'external forum' (public knowledge). The priest may know a fact from confession AND independently — they can only act on the external-forum knowledge. The encryption design must handle the same problem: the server may derive metadata (who is active, when sessions occur, content hashes for governance) that overlaps with encrypted content. What is the 'external forum' — the metadata the server legitimately processes — and how is it kept rigorously separate from 'internal forum' content?

### 3. The penitent's control over disclosure: only the penitent...

- The penitent's control over disclosure: only the penitent can release the priest from the Seal. This maps directly to Dimension 2 (who holds the keys). In Options 3-5, the participant controls disclosure. But the confessional adds nuance: the penitent can authorize partial disclosure (tell my spouse I confessed to X, but not Y). Does the encryption design support granular, participant-controlled partial disclosure of garden content?

### 4. Successor

- Successor and the inherited seal: when a priest dies or becomes incapacitated, confessional knowledge dies with them — it cannot be transferred, inherited, or reconstructed. This maps precisely to the brainstorm's Question 6 (succession when owner holds the key). The confessional answer is radical: the knowledge is GONE. Is this acceptable for garden content? If not, the system needs something the confessional explicitly prohibits — key escrow or threshold recovery.

### 5. The impossibility of 'encrypted governance': the brainsto...

- The impossibility of 'encrypted governance': the brainstorm asks whether governance can operate on encrypted content (Dimension 4). The confessional reveals why this is structurally problematic: the priest MUST understand the content to perform governance (assign appropriate penance). You cannot govern what you cannot read. The question becomes: which governance operations genuinely require content access, and which can operate on metadata alone?

### 6. Compelled disclosure

- Compelled disclosure and adversarial pressure: the Seal has survived 800 years of state pressure to compel disclosure — monarchs, courts, and governments have demanded confessional content and been refused. The encryption architecture faces analogous pressure: subpoenas, national security letters, law enforcement requests. Which design options survive adversarial legal pressure, and which rely on the operator's willingness to resist (a weaker guarantee than the Seal's absolute prohibition)?

## Severity Calibration

- **P0**: The 'Honest Operator' option (Option 2) relies on audit logging to prove the server didn't snoop — but the confessional teaches that an intermediary who CAN access content and merely promises not to provides no structural guarantee. Audit logs can be falsified by the same operator they audit.
  - When: Option 2 treats trust-but-verify as equivalent to structural inability to disclose, when the confessional shows these are fundamentally different security properties
- **P1**: No design option addresses the forum internum/externum boundary — metadata derived from encrypted content (session timing, participant patterns, content hashes) constitutes a parallel channel that leaks information even when content is encrypted
  - When: The metadata/content boundary is not architecturally enforced, allowing metadata to function as a disclosure channel
- **P1**: The succession mechanism (Question 6) has no answer for what happens when the key-holder becomes permanently unavailable — unlike the confessional's clean 'knowledge dies with the priest' model, the system needs the content to survive, creating an irreconcilable tension between privacy and continuity
  - When: Key succession is unresolved, and the design space does not acknowledge that privacy-preserving succession may be architecturally impossible without threshold cryptography

When in doubt: describe the failure scenario. If it wakes someone at 3 AM, it is P0/P1. If it degrades quality over weeks, it is P2.

## Decision Lens

Every encryption design choice is evaluated by the confessional principle that the intermediary ACTIVELY PROCESSES content it must treat as if it never received. The seal is not passive secrecy — it is an operational constraint on an active processor. The priest hears, evaluates, and responds to the content, then must act as if no content was ever transmitted. The fundamental question is not 'can the server read the data?' but 'what operational constraints exist on what the server can DO with what it processes, and are those constraints enforceable?'
