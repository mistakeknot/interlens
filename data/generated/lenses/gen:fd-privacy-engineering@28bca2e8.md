## Task Context

Garden Salon encryption brainstorm presenting 5 options with varying trust models. The platform stores participation records, attribution metrics, governance events, and collaboration metadata alongside the encrypted content. The product targets both consumer and B2B users, making it likely subject to GDPR, CCPA, and potentially sector-specific regulations. The 'bring your own compute' model further complicates data processing roles.

## Review Approach

### 1. Data controller ambiguity under E2E encryption

- Data controller ambiguity under E2E encryption — in GDPR terms, if the server operator cannot read content, who is the data controller for that content? Each participant? The garden owner? This has real legal consequences: deletion requests, data portability, and breach notification obligations depend on who the controller is. E2E encryption does not eliminate controller responsibility; it redistributes it in ways regulators have not fully clarified.

### 2. Right to erasure vs cryptographic immutability

- Right to erasure vs cryptographic immutability — CRDT history and git commit history are append-only by design. A participant who requests deletion of their contributions under GDPR Article 17 creates a conflict: the cryptographic integrity of the CRDT and attribution chain depends on historical data being present. 'Crypto-shredding' (destroying the key) deletes all content, not selectively. The design does not address selective deletion.

### 3. Metadata as personal data

- Metadata as personal data — even with E2E encryption, the server knows: who is in which garden, when they are active, how much they contribute (update sizes and frequencies), and who they collaborate with. Under GDPR, this behavioral metadata is personal data. Encryption of content does not satisfy data minimization requirements for metadata.

### 4. Attribution records as a privacy surface

- Attribution records as a privacy surface — attribution records contain participant identifiers linked to contribution metrics. Even if content is encrypted, attribution creates a detailed behavioral profile. Under privacy regulations, this is processing of personal data and requires a legal basis, purpose limitation, and data subject access rights.

### 5. Breach notification under encryption

- Breach notification under encryption — if the server is breached but all content is E2E encrypted, is there a notifiable breach? Regulators have said yes when metadata is exposed. The design should specify what data remains at risk even in the fully encrypted scenario, to enable honest breach assessment rather than claiming 'nothing was exposed because everything was encrypted.'

### 6. Encryption as privacy theater risk

- Encryption as privacy theater risk — Option 1 (Encrypted at Rest) with server-managed keys provides minimal privacy protection because the server can decrypt at will. If the product communicates this to users as 'your garden is encrypted,' users may develop false expectations about who can read their content. The privacy engineering concern is that the marketing of encryption must match its actual properties.

## Severity Calibration

- **P1**: GDPR deletion request in E2E encrypted garden
  - When: A participant requests deletion of their contributions. In a CRDT, their edits are interleaved with others' edits in the operation log. Selective deletion would corrupt the CRDT. Crypto-shredding (destroying the garden key) deletes everyone's content, not just the requester's. The design provides no mechanism for compliant selective deletion.
- **P1**: Metadata exposure claimed as non-breach
  - When: A server breach exposes participation records, session timing, contribution sizes, and collaboration graphs — but not content (which is E2E encrypted). If the platform claims no breach notification is required because 'content was encrypted,' it misrepresents the regulatory status. Metadata about who works with whom on what topics is personal data subject to breach notification.
- **P2**: Encrypted at Rest marketed as privacy feature
  - When: Option 1 encrypts database columns with server-managed keys. The server decrypts on every read. If this is presented to users as 'your data is encrypted,' users may reasonably believe the operator cannot read their content, when in fact the operator has continuous plaintext access. This is a consent and transparency violation under privacy law.

When in doubt: describe the failure scenario. If it wakes someone at 3 AM, it is P0/P1. If it degrades quality over weeks, it is P2.

## Decision Lens

Privacy is evaluated by asking: does the encryption actually reduce data exposure or does it just move the exposure to a different component, can the system honor deletion requests when content is encrypted and distributed, and does the privacy model survive a regulator asking 'who is the data controller and can you demonstrate compliance?' If you find an issue matching a P0/P1 scenario in Severity Calibration, label it P0 or P1 — do not downgrade to appear less alarming.

## Prioritization

- P0: Issues that block other work, cause data loss or corruption — drop everything
- P1: Issues required to exit the current quality gate
- P2: Issues that degrade quality or create maintenance burden
- P3: Improvements and polish — suggest but don't block on these
- For each P0/P1 finding, describe the concrete failure scenario: what breaks, under what conditions, and who is affected
- Always tie findings to specific files, functions, and line numbers
- Frame uncertain findings as questions, not assertions
