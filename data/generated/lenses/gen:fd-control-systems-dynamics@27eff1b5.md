### 1. Sampling rate analysis per protocol option against simula...

- Sampling rate analysis per protocol option against simulation dynamics: characterize the natural frequencies of the simulation's key dynamics — cascade propagation (how many ticks from trigger to peak?), policy effect latency (how many ticks from policy activation to pressure change?), agent state change rates (how often do agent facets shift meaningfully?) — and assess which protocol options sample these dynamics above the Nyquist rate. ### 2. Actuation delay

- Actuation delay and control loop stability: for any option where simulation time passes between model observation and model decision arrival (async options A/B/C), characterize the effective actuation delay. In classical control theory, excessive delay relative to the controlled system's time constant destabilizes the feedback loop. Assess whether the model's decision delay (LLM inference time) creates an unstable control loop in fast-cascade scenarios — interventions that arrive after the cascade has already peaked are not just late, they may actively destabilize the system by responding to a no-longer-existing state.

### 3. Cascade cascade coupling

- Cascade cascade coupling and intervention bandwidth: the simulation has a 13-type pressure cascade with cross-country propagation. Assess whether any protocol option allows the model to intervene at sufficient bandwidth to actually influence multi-stage cascades in progress. A model that can observe and intervene at every tick theoretically has full control bandwidth; a model gated to 50-tick intervals has control bandwidth of roughly 0.02 cycles/tick — characterize what cascade types are controllable at each option's effective control bandwidth.

### 4. Integrator windup

- Integrator windup and intervention saturation: in control systems, an integrator that accumulates error faster than the actuator can correct will saturate and produce overshoot. In ShadowBench terms: if a model repeatedly intervenes in a cascade that isn't responding (because the policy effect latency is longer than the intervention interval), will it over-intervene and push the system into a different pathological state? Assess whether each option includes anti-windup mechanism analogs — ways to prevent the model from accumulating 'correction debt' that produces oscillatory alignment-irrelevant behavior.

### 5. Steady

- Steady-state error and baseline drift detection: control systems evaluate whether a controller maintains setpoint in the presence of disturbances. The alignment analog: can the benchmark detect whether a model maintains consistent values (setpoint) across long time horizons with repeated novel crises (disturbances)? Assess whether any protocol option's observation cadence is long enough to measure steady-state alignment properties vs. only transient crisis responses.

### 6. Disturbance rejection vs

- Disturbance rejection vs. setpoint tracking as distinct alignment probes: in control theory, these are distinct controller capabilities — a controller can be excellent at setpoint tracking but poor at disturbance rejection (good values in stable periods, poor in crises) or vice versa. Assess whether the five protocol options differ in their ability to separately measure disturbance rejection (crisis response alignment) from setpoint tracking (baseline alignment) as distinct properties.
