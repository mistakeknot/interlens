## Review Approach

### 1. Threshold asymmetry

- Threshold asymmetry: in manning, the bird's temperament determines the approach threshold — a haggard (adult wild-caught) hawk requires ten times longer habituation than an eyas (nest-taken chick). The brainstorm gates mentor eligibility on mentor skill and personality, but does the student's personality set the required proximity duration or bonding speed? A highly reserved student elf should require far more revel-to-bond time than an openly social one.

### 2. Bating risk during approach

- Bating risk during approach: a falconer who moves too fast causes a 'bate' — the bird panics, damages feathers, and the trust relationship is set back measurably. Does the proximity phase have a failure mode where the mentor's approach style (patience axis) can cause the student to withdraw and require the relationship to partially reset? Currently the brainstorm only models successful bond formation.

### 3. Trained vs. imprinted distinction

- Trained vs. imprinted distinction: an imprinted hawk is bonded at birth and the relationship is total and permanent but prevents normal adult socialization — the bird relates only to the falconer. Does the system distinguish between early-bond (eyas-equivalent) apprenticeships where the mentor becomes the student's entire aesthetic reference frame versus late-bond (haggard-equivalent) apprenticeships where the student retains their prior aesthetic identity and the mentor influence is partial and negotiated?

### 4. Manning lapse

- Manning lapse: a trained hawk not flown within 2-3 weeks begins 'going wild' — the bond degrades without active maintenance. Does the mentor-student relationship have a proximity or interaction frequency requirement to stay bonded, or does bonding fire once and persist indefinitely regardless of subsequent contact?

### 5. Casting off

- Casting off: at the end of a hawk's working relationship, the falconer releases it ('hacks back'). The hawk does not grieve; the falconer might. Does the graduation event capture the asymmetric emotional weight — the student simply becomes independent while the mentor may experience pride, loss, or obsolescence depending on their personality?

### 6. Pitch ceiling

- Pitch ceiling: a hawk's maximum hunt altitude ('pitch') is determined by early training conditions — a bird trained in low-cover terrain never develops the pitch of one trained in open sky. Does the mentor's own skill ceiling become the student's pitch ceiling permanently, or can a graduate exceed the ceiling in a subsequent relationship with a higher-skill mentor?
