## Task Context

Reviewing the encryption design brainstorm for Garden Salon. The brainstorm presents five options spanning encrypted-at-rest to full E2E, plus a hybrid (Sealed Rooms) where per-garden encryption policy creates gardens with different trust models. The core UX challenge: encryption adds invisible infrastructure that participants must trust, and the hybrid model requires participants to maintain two different mental models.

## Review Approach

### 1. The kurogo protocol

- The kurogo protocol and encryption-as-convention: Bunraku's invisibility is a SOCIAL PROTOCOL, not a physical property. The puppeteers wear black, move slowly, and avoid eye contact — but they are fully visible. E2E encryption similarly relies on participants trusting that the mathematical protocol makes the server 'invisible' even though the server physically handles every byte. How does the design communicate to participants that the relay server handling their encrypted CRDT updates is 'wearing kurogo' — present but conventionally invisible?

### 2. The omozukai exception

- The omozukai exception — the visible operator: the main puppeteer shows their face and wears formal clothing, visibly taking artistic credit. In the encryption design, the AI proxy (Dimension 3) is the omozukai — it MUST be visible because it processes plaintext. Option 3 (Blind Relay) eliminates the omozukai entirely, losing artistic capability. Option 4 (Client-Side AI) moves the omozukai into the audience (client-side). Is there a Bunraku-like design where the AI proxy is the acknowledged visible operator — participants see it and consent to its role — while the relay infrastructure remains kurogo-invisible?

### 3. Convention

- Convention-breaking moments: in Bunraku, the illusion breaks when a puppeteer makes a sudden movement, drops something, or is poorly coordinated with the other two operators. In encryption, the illusion breaks when the user sees infrastructure — a loading spinner that says 'decrypting...', an error message that reveals server-side processing, a feature limitation that exposes the encryption boundary. What convention-breaking moments exist in each design option, and how catastrophic is each break?

### 4. The three

- The three-operator coordination problem: each Bunraku puppet requires three puppeteers in precise coordination — main operator (head/right arm), left-arm operator, and leg operator. If one is out of sync, the puppet's movement becomes uncanny. In the encryption design, the 'puppet' (seamless collaboration experience) requires coordination between the CRDT layer, the encryption layer, and the governance layer. Options 3-5 add encryption as a fourth operator. What happens when encryption introduces latency or failure modes that make the collaboration feel uncanny — not broken, but subtly wrong?

### 5. The audience's learned perception

- The audience's learned perception and onboarding: Bunraku audiences learn the kurogo convention gradually — first-time viewers often cannot stop seeing the puppeteers. Experienced viewers genuinely do not see them. How does the encryption design onboard participants into perceiving encryption correctly? First-time users of an E2E encrypted garden will likely feel anxiety ('is my data safe?') or confusion ('why can't I search?'). What is the Bunraku equivalent — the slow acculturation to perceiving encryption as a natural, invisible part of the experience?

### 6. The two

- The two-stage problem in Sealed Rooms (Option 5): Bunraku sometimes uses mixed conventions — some characters are puppets, others are live actors on the same stage. This is extremely difficult to execute because the audience must hold two perceptual frames simultaneously. Option 5 (Sealed Rooms) creates exactly this problem: some gardens are encrypted (puppets operated by invisible kurogo) and some are open (live actors). Can participants maintain two different trust models for different gardens without confusion?

## Severity Calibration

- **P1**: The UI exposes encryption infrastructure during normal operation — loading states that reveal decryption, error messages that reference server-side processing, or feature differences between encrypted and unencrypted gardens that make the encryption boundary the dominant perceptual element rather than an invisible convention
  - When: Encryption is perceptible as friction rather than invisible as protocol — the audience sees the puppeteer instead of the puppet
- **P1**: Option 5 (Sealed Rooms) creates a two-class experience where encrypted gardens feel degraded — fewer features, slower performance, visible limitations — making encryption feel like a cost rather than a right. Participants choose 'open' not because they trust the operator but because 'sealed' is a worse experience.
  - When: The mixed-convention problem: participants cannot hold two trust models simultaneously, and the encrypted model is perceptibly inferior
- **P2**: The coordination between CRDT sync, encryption, and governance introduces subtle uncanny-valley effects — collaboration that is not broken but feels slightly wrong (fractional-second delays in cursor sync, governance ceremonies that take slightly longer, search that returns slightly different results)
  - When: The three-operator coordination breaks down at the edges, creating an uncanny collaboration experience that erodes trust without producing identifiable failures

When in doubt: describe the failure scenario. If it wakes someone at 3 AM, it is P0/P1. If it degrades quality over weeks, it is P2.

## Decision Lens

Every encryption-boundary design choice is evaluated by the Bunraku principle that invisibility is a PERCEPTUAL PROTOCOL, not a physical absence. The puppeteers are present, visible, and actively operating — the audience's trained attention renders them invisible. Encryption in a collaborative system works the same way: the encrypted data passes through servers, relays, and proxies that are physically present. The question is not 'is the server there?' but 'does the participant perceive the server's handling of their content, and does that perception create trust or anxiety?' The kurogo convention only works when ALL operators follow the same protocol — one operator breaking convention destroys the illusion for the entire stage. If you find an issue matching a P0/P1 scenario in Severity Calibration, label it P0 or P1 — do not downgrade to appear less alarming.
