Current rendering uses simple single-character glyphs (. for meadow, # for stone, ~ for water, H/W/G/F for buildings, first-letter for elves) with basic Color enum values (Green, Blue, White, etc.). The core tension: how to convey rich, living worlds through the extremely constrained medium of a character grid.

## Review Approach

### 1. Intentional reduction vs

- Intentional reduction vs. resolution hunger: wayang kulit proves that the most constrained visual medium (solid black silhouettes on white) can convey armies, forests, palaces, and emotional nuance — IF the reduction is intentional rather than accidental. The impulse toward half-blocks, braille, and sixel graphics is resolution hunger — the assumption that MORE PIXELS = BETTER. The wayang insight suggests the opposite direction: what if each character cell conveyed MORE meaning by showing LESS detail? A meadow tile that is just '.' in green is already a shadow puppet — it communicates 'meadow' instantly. Does the enrichment plan distinguish between cases where more resolution genuinely serves comprehension (detailed map views) vs. where it adds noise (terrain that was already legible)?

### 2. Lamp

- Lamp-distance depth and the fg/bg color channel as z-axis: in wayang, the only depth cue is shadow sharpness — puppets near the screen (foreground) are crisp and dark, puppets far away are soft and light. Terminal cells have exactly two color channels (foreground and background) that could encode this same depth grammar. High-contrast fg/bg = foreground/important. Low-contrast fg/bg = background/ambient. Does the color design use contrast ratio as a systematic depth signal, or does each element choose colors for individual aesthetics without a global contrast grammar? The wayang dalang never lets a background puppet cast a shadow as sharp as a foreground one.

### 3. The kayon principle

- The kayon principle — context-dependent glyph semantics: the kayon (tree of life) puppet means 'forest' when placed at the side, 'scene change' when swept across, 'mountain' when tilted, and 'cosmic axis' in the opening. One physical object, five+ meanings through context. ASCII glyphs should work the same way — '|' could mean wall, tree trunk, rain, or division depending on what surrounds it. Does the rendering design allow context-dependent glyph interpretation (the same character means different things based on neighbors, color, and position), or is each glyph permanently assigned to one meaning? A wayang-informed design would have a SMALL glyph vocabulary with RICH contextual grammar.

### 4. The dalang as orchestrator

- The dalang as orchestrator and the render pipeline as performance: the dalang simultaneously manipulates puppets, voices characters, cues music, and manages pacing — a single intelligence creating a multi-channel experience from minimal resources. The render pipeline should similarly orchestrate character, color, timing, and spatial composition as coordinated channels of a single performance, not independent subsystems. Does the rendering architecture have a 'dalang' — a composition layer that coordinates glyph selection, color assignment, animation timing, and information priority into a coherent visual performance? Or are these independent systems that may conflict (an important event rendered in a color that accidentally matches the background)?

### 5. Scene structure

- Scene structure and information layering — the three-act puppet layout: wayang performances have a formal spatial structure — refined characters on the right of the screen, rough characters on the left, the kayon in the center. The audience reads character nature from screen position before seeing any detail. The 120x40 grid has analogous spatial zones (map area, sidebar, footer, event strip) that should follow a consistent information architecture. Does the rendering plan define spatial zones with consistent semantic meaning (e.g., left = world state, right = character state, bottom = events), or does each screen element choose its own placement ad hoc?

### 6. Transitional effects

- Transitional effects and the kayon sweep: wayang scene transitions use the kayon sweeping across the screen — a dramatic, legible, and beautiful moment created with one puppet and one gesture. Terminal 'transitions' between states (pausing, zooming, mode changes) could use analogous single-glyph sweep effects rather than instant state changes. Does the design consider that transitions between render states are themselves an opportunity for expression, or are state changes instantaneous and invisible?

## Severity Calibration

- **P1**: The enrichment plan adds resolution (half-blocks, braille) uniformly across all render elements without distinguishing which elements benefit from more detail and which are already optimally legible as simple glyphs — adding visual noise to currently clean signals
  - When: Half-block or braille rendering is proposed for terrain that is already perfectly legible as single-character glyphs, with no framework for deciding WHEN increased resolution serves comprehension vs. when it degrades it
