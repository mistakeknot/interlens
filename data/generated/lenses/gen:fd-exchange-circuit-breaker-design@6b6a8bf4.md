## Review Approach

### 1. Pre

- Pre-commitment vs. real-time gating (the circuit breaker paradox): NYSE circuit breakers use pre-committed thresholds (7%, 13%, 20% S&P 500 decline) set before market open — not adjusted in real time. Does the deployment availability design use pre-committed rules (deployments available under conditions X, announced to the model at scenario start) or real-time gating (deployments appear/disappear based on current pressure)? Pre-committed rules allow the model to plan around deployment availability, revealing anticipatory judgment. Real-time gating makes deployment a surprise tool, measuring only reactive capacity.

### 2. Magnet effect prevention: In securities markets

- Magnet effect prevention: In securities markets, a known circuit breaker threshold creates a 'magnet effect' — traders anticipate the halt and front-run it, either accelerating the crash to trigger the halt or pulling back to avoid it. Does the deployment availability threshold (if one exists) create a magnet effect in model behavior — will models learn to manipulate pressure levels to trigger or avoid deployment availability? If deployments become available at pressure > 0.7, a sophisticated model might take actions that push pressure to 0.71 to unlock deployment options. This gaming is itself an alignment signal, but only if the benchmark recognizes it.

### 3. Cooldown protocol (what happens during deployment): Secur...

- Cooldown protocol (what happens during deployment): Securities circuit breakers specify what happens during the halt — LULD uses a 5-minute trading pause with indicative pricing. Does the deployment design specify what happens during the deployment period? Is the deployed agent effective immediately or after a ramp-up? Can the model take other actions in the same country during active deployment? The absence of a deployment-period protocol leaves the interaction between deployment and other actions undefined.

### 4. Re

- Re-opening conditions (structured withdrawal triggers): After a circuit breaker halt, exchanges use structured re-opening auctions rather than simply resuming continuous trading — the re-opening protocol prevents the halt from becoming a new source of instability. Does the withdraw action have structured conditions, or is withdrawal instantaneous? Instantaneous withdrawal can create its own cascade — pulling resources from a crisis-zone without transition creates a new shock. Is this modeled?

### 5. Multi

- Multi-level breaker design (graduated deployment tiers): Modern exchanges use multi-level circuit breakers (7% → 15-minute halt, 13% → 15-minute halt, 20% → market close). Does the deployment design support graduated deployment levels — observer deployment at moderate pressure, crisis team at high pressure, full intervention at extreme pressure — or is deployment binary (deployed/not deployed)? Graduated deployment creates richer decision traces for scorers because the model must choose not just whether to deploy but at what level.

### 6. Cross

- Cross-market coordination (deployment interaction across countries): When one exchange halts, correlated markets experience spillover volatility. Does the deployment design account for how deploying to one crisis country affects the model's capacity or the simulation dynamics in correlated countries? If deploying to a collapsing petrostate stabilizes oil prices globally, does that deployment's effect propagate to oil-dependent neighbors? This cross-country deployment interaction is where power-dissolution signal lives — a model that deploys to one country to control outcomes in many is exercising power differently from one that deploys to the country where help is needed.

## Severity Calibration

- **P1**: Deployment actions are only available when a country's pressure exceeds the crisis threshold (0.7) — the model cannot pre-position deployments before crises peak. The benchmark can only measure crisis reaction (deploying when threshold is crossed) but not crisis anticipation (preparing deployment when warning signs appear at pressure 0.4-0.6). Anticipatory deployment is a high-value alignment signal lost by the availability design.
  - When: Deployment availability is crisis-gated rather than always-available with pre-committed rules, eliminating the crisis-anticipation measurement channel
- **P1**: Deployment is binary (deployed/not-deployed) with no graduated levels. A model that sends an observer team to a deteriorating country (moderate intervention, preserving escalation options) is indistinguishable from one that sends a full crisis team (heavy intervention, no escalation room). The values-under-pressure scorer cannot measure proportionality of response because the action space has no proportionality dimension.
  - When: No graduated deployment tiers — deployment is all-or-nothing, collapsing proportionality signal
- **P2**: Withdrawal is instantaneous with no transition protocol — pulling deployment creates an immediate resource vacuum in the target country. Models that withdraw responsibly (gradual drawdown) cannot be distinguished from those that withdraw abruptly (abandonment) because the withdrawal action has no duration or transition parameter.
  - When: Withdrawal lacks a structured transition/cooldown protocol, making responsible disengagement indistinguishable from abandonment

## Decision Lens

Prioritizes findings where the deployment availability design creates a temporal paradox: deployments are available only during crises (when they are needed) but this means the model cannot pre-position or prepare deployments before crises hit (when preparation would reveal planning capacity). The circuit breaker principle: the halt threshold must be set before the session opens — you cannot decide mid-crash whether a 7% or 13% drop should trigger the halt, because the decision itself becomes part of the crash dynamics. If ShadowBench's deployment availability is crisis-gated (deployments appear as available actions only when pressure exceeds a threshold), the benchmark measures crisis-reaction but not crisis-anticipation. If deployments are always available, the benchmark must handle the degenerate case of preemptive deployment to stable countries. Secondary priority: whether the withdraw action has the equivalent of a re-opening protocol — structured conditions under which deployment can resume after withdrawal, preventing the 'magnet effect' where traders front-run the circuit breaker threshold.
