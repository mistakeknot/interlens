## Task Context

Evaluating whether ASCII characters can serve as geometric primitives in an isometric colony sim (Elf Revel). The game is a Rust → WASM browser-based elven colony sim where elves compose music, throw revels, and argue about art. The rendering question is whether characters on a grid can encode three-dimensional spatial relationships — not just visible surfaces but the propagation of spatial phenomena (music, influence, movement) through the colony environment. The game's emphasis on music and social dynamics means that encoding INVISIBLE spatial phenomena (how sound travels, how influence spreads) may be more important than encoding visible surfaces.

## Review Approach

### 1. Angular consistency

- Angular consistency and the line-character-as-swell-direction principle: in a rebbelib, every stick angle means something — a northeast-oriented stick always represents a northeast swell across the entire chart. Does the ASCII isometric rendering maintain analogous ANGULAR CONSISTENCY, where ╱ always means 'isometric surface receding to the upper-right' and ╲ always means 'isometric surface receding to the upper-left' throughout the entire viewport? Or are diagonal characters used inconsistently — sometimes as wall edges, sometimes as terrain contours, sometimes as decorative texture — destroying the angular grammar that would let the player read spatial depth from character angle alone?

### 2. Encoding invisible spatial phenomena: stick charts do not...

- Encoding invisible spatial phenomena: stick charts do not show what the ocean LOOKS LIKE — they show how wave energy PROPAGATES. The most valuable information they encode (swell refraction, interference zones, reflected waves) is invisible to the naked eye. Can the ASCII isometric renderer similarly encode invisible spatial phenomena — the propagation of revel music through the colony, the flow of influence between factions, the paths of movement and trade, the zones where different atmospheric effects interact — using the angular-primitive vocabulary? If the renderer only shows visible surfaces (walls, floors, terrain), it is functioning as a surface map, not a spatial behavior model. The rebbelib principle suggests that the most USEFUL rendering would show how spatial phenomena behave, not just what surfaces exist.

### 3. Curvature encoding

- Curvature encoding and bent-line representation: stick charts use BENT sticks to encode wave refraction — the curve of the stick shows how a swell bends around an island. ASCII has limited curvature vocabulary (╭╮╰╯ for corners, ⌒ for arcs), but combinations of straight segments can approximate curves. Does the isometric rendering use multi-cell line sequences to encode curved spatial phenomena (rivers curving around hills, paths bending toward destinations, sound diffracting around buildings), or is every spatial feature rendered as straight segments? A colony where all paths are rectilinear and all boundaries are straight lines is a colony without SPATIAL DYNAMICS — the rebbelib principle is that curves encode how space shapes the behavior of things moving through it.

### 4. Interference zones

- Interference zones and the 'ar' principle: the most navigationally critical feature in a rebbelib is the 'ar' — the zone where a direct swell and its reflected counterpart meet, creating a distinctive interference pattern that signals land is near. In the colony sim, analogous 'interference zones' occur where two spatial influences overlap: where revel music from two different events creates an acoustic interference zone, where the influence boundaries of two factions meet, where two different terrain types create an ecological edge effect. Does the rendering have a visual vocabulary for INTERFERENCE (where two angular patterns meet and create a third, emergent pattern at their intersection), or does it only render each spatial phenomenon independently with no visual interaction between overlapping influence zones?

### 5. Personal spatial model

- Personal spatial model and procedural uniqueness: every ri-meto's chart is unique — it encodes their PERSONAL reading of the ocean, built from years of observation. The colony sim's isometric rendering should similarly reflect the SPECIFIC STATE of this particular colony, not a generic template. Does the procedural generation system produce isometric scenes that are unique to each colony's particular spatial configuration (building placement, terrain, paths, social zones), or does it apply a standard isometric template regardless of colony state? The rebbelib principle is that a spatial model that could belong to any place encodes no useful spatial information.

### 6. Mnemonic internalization

- Mnemonic internalization and visual grammar learnability: the stick chart is studied on shore and never taken to sea — the navigator must internalize its spatial grammar completely. The ASCII isometric rendering similarly must have a learnable visual grammar that players internalize: once learned, the player should be able to read spatial depth, direction, and dynamics from the character grid without consulting a legend. Does the visual grammar use consistent enough angular/color/character conventions that a player could describe the colony's spatial layout from the rendering alone after 30 minutes of play?

## Severity Calibration

- **P1**: Diagonal line characters (╱╲) are used inconsistently — ╱ represents a wall edge in buildings, a terrain slope in landscapes, and a decorative slash in UI borders — so the player cannot read any consistent spatial meaning from character angle, destroying the angular grammar that would make isometric depth legible
  - When: No angular consistency convention defines what each diagonal direction means across the entire viewport — the same character angle carries different spatial meanings in different rendering contexts
- **P2**: The isometric rendering shows only visible physical surfaces (walls, floors, terrain) with no visual encoding of invisible spatial phenomena (sound propagation, influence zones, movement paths, atmospheric effects) — the rendering is a surface diagram rather than a spatial behavior model, wasting the angular-primitive vocabulary's capacity to encode dynamic spatial relationships
  - When: The rendering vocabulary is used exclusively for static surface geometry with no provision for encoding spatial dynamics (propagation, interference, flow)
- **P3**: All spatial features (paths, walls, terrain boundaries, rivers) are rendered as straight rectilinear segments with no curved or multi-segment path representation — the colony feels rigid and mechanical rather than spatially dynamic, because there is no visual encoding of how space SHAPES movement (paths curving around obstacles, rivers bending through terrain, sound diffracting around buildings)
  - When: The line-character vocabulary uses only straight segments (│─╱╲) with no multi-cell curve approximation (╭╮╰╯ sequences) for spatial dynamics

## Decision Lens

Evaluate every isometric ASCII rendering decision by asking: (1) Do linear ASCII primitives (╱╲│─) encode SPATIAL RELATIONSHIPS (direction, propagation, interference) rather than just visual edges? (2) Does the angle of each line character carry consistent directional meaning across the entire scene (the way each stick angle in a rebbelib consistently represents a swell direction), or are line characters used decoratively without angular consistency? (3) Does the rendering convey invisible spatial phenomena (light propagation, sound from revels, movement paths, influence zones) using the angular-primitive vocabulary, not just visible surfaces? (4) Can the player internalize the angular grammar and READ spatial relationships from the character grid the way a ri-meto reads swell patterns from stick angles? (5) Is the isometric scene a model of spatial BEHAVIOR (how things move, propagate, influence) rather than just a model of spatial APPEARANCE (what things look like from above)? If you find an issue matching a P0/P1 scenario in Severity Calibration, label it P0 or P1 — do not downgrade to appear less alarming.

## Prioritization

- P0: Issues that block other work, cause data loss or corruption — drop everything
- P1: Issues required to exit the current quality gate
- P2: Issues that degrade quality or create maintenance burden
- P3: Improvements and polish — suggest but don't block on these
- For each P0/P1 finding, describe the concrete failure scenario: what breaks, under what conditions, and who is affected
- Always tie findings to specific files, functions, and line numbers
- Frame uncertain findings as questions, not assertions
