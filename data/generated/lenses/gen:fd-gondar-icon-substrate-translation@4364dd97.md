## Review Approach

### 1. Essential vs

- Essential vs. substrate-bound features of ASCII identity: Gondarine painters identified that the frontal gaze, color coding, and register system were THEOLOGICALLY essential — they carried meaning independent of substrate. Fine gold leaf and tight borders were SUBSTRATE-BOUND — they existed because parchment afforded them, not because they carried independent meaning. ASCII has analogous essential features (grid regularity, character-as-semantic-unit, color-attribute system, constraint-born readability) and substrate-bound features (single-character-per-cell, monochrome limitation, terminal-specific escape codes). Does the renderer decision explicitly categorize which ASCII features are identity-essential versus which are substrate artifacts of terminal rendering? Rich multi-pixel tiles should preserve the essential features while being free to abandon the substrate-bound ones.

### 2. Viewing distance transformation: a manuscript is read at ...

- Viewing distance transformation: a manuscript is read at 30cm with focused attention on sequential details. A church wall is perceived at 3-8 meters with ambient attention on simultaneous relationships. The Gondarine painters adapted: they enlarged eyes (the primary carrier of sacred presence) disproportionately, simplified drapery, and strengthened color contrast — all because the new viewing distance demanded different emphasis. The ASCII-to-browser transition has an analogous viewing distance change: terminal text is read at close range with focused attention on individual characters, while a browser game map is perceived at variable zoom with ambient attention on patterns. Does the renderer plan account for how the viewing context changes what needs emphasis? Rich tiles at browser scale may need to emphasize different features than ASCII characters at terminal scale.

### 3. The register system

- The register system and spatial grammar preservation: Gondarine church paintings preserve the manuscript's register system (horizontal bands reading top-to-bottom) even when the wall's circular shape fights against rectangular registers. They bent the essential spatial grammar to fit the new substrate rather than abandoning it. ASCII colony sims have a spatial grammar: the grid, the layering order (terrain → entity → UI), the color coding conventions. If the renderer moves to rich multi-pixel tiles, does the spatial grammar survive the substrate change? Or does the new substrate's freedom (pixel-level positioning, arbitrary tile sizes) tempt the design away from the grid discipline that made ASCII spatially legible?

### 4. Narrative program vs

- Narrative program vs. individual icon — system coherence: a single Gondarine icon (one saint on the wall) must be recognizable. But the PROGRAM (the full church interior as a coherent theological narrative) matters more than any individual icon. Translating individual icons successfully while breaking the program's coherence is a failure. Similarly: individual ASCII tiles rendered as rich multi-pixel art might each look excellent, but the PROGRAM — the full map as a coherent visual system where every tile works together — determines whether the renderer succeeds. Does the renderer decision evaluate tiles as a system (do they tessellate coherently?) or as individuals (does each tile look good in isolation)?

### 5. Candlelight

- Candlelight and degradation tolerance: Gondarine wall paintings are designed to be viewed in dim, flickering candlelight — they degrade gracefully under poor viewing conditions because the essential identity markers (frontal gaze, color coding) are robust enough to survive luminance reduction. Terminal ASCII is similarly degradation-tolerant: it works in any terminal, at any font size, under any color scheme that preserves attribute distinctions. Rich multi-pixel tiles may lose this degradation tolerance — they may require specific resolution, color depth, or rendering fidelity to remain legible. Does the renderer plan preserve the degradation tolerance of ASCII (works in any browser, at any zoom, under any color scheme) or does it introduce fragility that the old substrate didn't have?

### 6. The translation team structure

- The translation team structure — who does the adaptation work: Gondarine church painting was not done by manuscript illuminators working bigger. It was done by a distinct craft tradition that UNDERSTOOD the manuscript canon but worked with different tools, scales, and constraints. The implication for the renderer decision: the person/team adapting Bevy or building a custom renderer should understand ASCII aesthetic conventions deeply but work with renderer-native thinking, not try to make a terminal emulator that happens to use GPU tiles. Is the adaptation plan structured as a genuine substrate translation (new craft skills applied to preserved identity) or as a scaling exercise (same techniques made bigger)?

## Severity Calibration

- **P1**: The renderer plan treats the ASCII-to-rich-tile transition as pure visual upgrade ('better graphics') without identifying which ASCII features carry essential identity (must be preserved in any renderer) versus which are terminal-substrate artifacts (can be abandoned), risking a result where individual tiles look impressive but the overall visual system no longer reads as the same 'text'
  - When: No explicit categorization of essential vs. substrate-bound ASCII features exists in the design, and the tile enrichment plan implicitly assumes all ASCII features are limitations to overcome
- **P2**: Rich multi-pixel tiles are designed for optimal-condition viewing (high-res display, specific zoom level, full color depth) without degradation tolerance testing, introducing fragility that the ASCII substrate never had — the equivalent of painting a church icon that is only legible in direct sunlight
  - When: The renderer plan does not specify minimum viable rendering conditions (low resolution, high contrast mode, small window, mobile browser) and does not test tile legibility under degraded conditions
- **P2**: Tiles are evaluated individually for visual quality without testing systemic coherence — each tile looks good but the full map fails to read as a coherent visual program, like individually excellent icons that break the church's narrative flow
  - When: Tile design review evaluates individual tile aesthetics without full-map tessellation testing at the intended viewing scale

When in doubt: describe the failure scenario. If it wakes someone at 3 AM, it is P0/P1. If it degrades quality over weeks, it is P2.

Does the translation plan account for the change in viewing conditions (distance, duration, context) that the new substrate imposes? Is the identity model explicit enough that a literate 'reader' of the old medium would recognize the same 'text' in the new medium?
