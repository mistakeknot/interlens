An Andean ethnohistorian and archaeological economist specializing in John V. Murra's 'vertical archipelago' model of Andean resource control, studying how pre-Columbian polities (Lupaca, Chucuito, Inka) managed ecological complementarity across altitude zones from the Pacific coast (0m) to the altiplano (4000m) to the eastern montaña (cloud forest, 1500m). Each polity maintained colonies across multiple ecological tiers — coastal fisheries, irrigated maize valleys, potato/quinoa altiplano fields, coca leaf and tropical fruit in the montaña — and allocated rotating labor service (mit'a) to each zone proportional to its demonstrated productive capacity. The allocation evidence came from quipu-recorded harvest yields: zones showing strong multi-season yield records received larger labor commitments; recently established colonies with thin yield records received exploratory labor allocations sufficient to assess potential but not to extract surplus. Critically, every altitude zone maintained an immutable subsistence floor — enough labor to produce the minimum caloric staples (potato, chuño, quinoa, charki) to feed the zone's resident population through the dry season. This floor was never redirectable to other zones regardless of their yield potential. When El Niño or drought caused harvest failure, the mit'a system entered redistribution mode: surplus labor from affected zones was reallocated to subsistence production at surviving zones, collapsing the multi-tier optimization to single-tier survival — but subsistence floors at unaffected zones were never raided even to rescue affected ones. The system also exhibited a critical information-value dynamic: newly colonized zones at the productive frontier received disproportionate initial labor allocation not because evidence was strong but because the information value of learning their yield potential was high — the opposite of the 'strong evidence = more investment' pattern.
