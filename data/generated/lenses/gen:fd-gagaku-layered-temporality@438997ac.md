## Task Context

Reviewing the UX philosophy of Garden Salon, a multiplayer human-agent collaboration workspace. The ceremony system uses silence-as-consent with explicit objection. CRDT layer handles real-time state. Governance layer authorizes state transitions. The fundamental temporal challenge is that agents operate at millisecond timescales and humans at hour/day timescales.

## Review Approach

### 1. Temporal strata separation: humans

- Temporal strata separation: humans and agents operate at fundamentally different speeds — does the UX treat this as a feature (like gagaku's layered temporality) or as a bug to be hidden? An agent that completes work in 3 seconds and a human who reviews over 3 days should each feel like they are operating at a natural pace

### 2. The shō problem

- The shō problem — sustaining harmonic ground: in gagaku the shō provides aitake (tone clusters) that persist across all other activity, creating a continuous tonal environment. What is the UX equivalent of this sustained harmonic ground — the always-present ambient state that makes activity legible without demanding attention?

### 3. Ma (negative space) as a first

- Ma (negative space) as a first-class UX element: gagaku's power comes from the spaces BETWEEN notes, not the notes themselves. Does the ceremony system's silence-as-consent feel like ma (generative, pregnant, meaningful) or like absence (empty, broken, waiting)?

### 4. Netori

- Netori — the ritual opening that establishes tonal space: gagaku begins with netori, a free-rhythm introduction where instruments tune to each other and establish the mode. Does entering a garden have a netori phase where participants orient to the current state before structured work begins?

### 5. Jo

- Jo-ha-kyū pacing arc: gagaku follows a jo (slow introduction) → ha (breaking, development) → kyū (rapid conclusion) arc at every structural level. Does a garden session have this arc, or is it flat? Does the UX support different pacing for different phases of collaborative work?

### 6. Orchestral heterophony vs

- Orchestral heterophony vs. unison: gagaku instruments play the 'same' melody but each in their own temporal interpretation — this is heterophony, not harmony. Does the UX allow participants to be working on the same thing in genuinely different ways simultaneously, or does it force either unison (everyone sees the same) or isolation (everyone is separate)?

## Severity Calibration

- **P1**: The interface treats human and agent temporal rhythms as requiring synchronization — e.g., blocking an agent from starting new work until a human reviews, or presenting agent output at agent-speed rather than at a pace the human temporal stratum can absorb
  - When: The UX forces all participants into a single shared temporal stratum instead of supporting layered coexistence
- **P1**: Silence in the ceremony system feels like a timeout or a broken state rather than a meaningful interval — the UI shows 'waiting for votes' with a countdown rather than treating the silent period as active, generative space
  - When: Ma (meaningful silence) is presented as absence or delay rather than as a first-class temporal element
- **P2**: Entering a garden is immediate and transactional — no netori phase where participants orient to the current state, feel the active work, and attune before contributing
  - When: The transition from outside to inside a garden has no orientation ritual

When in doubt: describe the failure scenario. If it wakes someone at 3 AM, it is P0/P1. If it degrades quality over weeks, it is P2.

## Decision Lens

Every temporal aspect of the interface is evaluated by the gagaku principle that different participants operate in genuinely different temporal strata — not synchronized to a shared clock, but creating coherence through layered overlapping cycles. The shō holds the tonal ground (harmonic aitake clusters that persist across beats), the ryūteki melody floats above in a different rhythmic relationship, and the percussion marks structural boundaries at a third timescale. The question is never 'are they in sync?' but 'do the layers create a felt whole?' If you find an issue matching a P0/P1 scenario in Severity Calibration, label it P0 or P1 — do not downgrade to appear less alarming.

## Prioritization

- P0: Issues that block other work, cause data loss or corruption — drop everything
- P1: Issues required to exit the current quality gate
- P2: Issues that degrade quality or create maintenance burden
- P3: Improvements and polish — suggest but don't block on these
- For each P0/P1 finding, describe the concrete failure scenario: what breaks, under what conditions, and who is affected
- Always tie findings to specific files, functions, and line numbers
- Frame uncertain findings as questions, not assertions
