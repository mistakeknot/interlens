## Severity Calibration

- **P0**: The one deliverable that lies across a seam has no side to fall on
  - When: DONE WHEN requires one fallen civilization whose ruins and records are queryable in the present at a real location, evidenced by the event log itself. That object is not a part of any one stratum. Its coordinates belong to the substrate; its builders belong to the civ stratum and are extinct; its evidentiary existence belongs to the history stratum's log; and its queryability in the present belongs to whatever runs after generation stops. A butcher looking at a body whose chart has four cuts and a required piece that spans three of them knows the chart is wrong before he has picked up a knife, because there is no way to take that piece off without taking it out of somewhere. The condition never assigns the ruin an owner. Each stratum can be built to specification and the ruin will still be nobody's responsibility, which means it will be produced by whichever stage happens to notice, in whatever form that stage finds convenient, and the acceptance criterion will be satisfied by an artifact with no stable home in the architecture the whole goal exists to establish.
- **P0**: Three nouns and a verb, called four strata
  - When: OUTCOME states the pipeline in pace-layer order as galaxy scaffold, then species stubs, then civilizations of member Souls, then simulated deep history. The first three are kinds of thing that exist in a world; the fourth is a process that runs over all of them. They are not four cuts of one body, and the joints between them are not comparable joints: the boundary between stars and species is a boundary between two populations of entities, while the boundary between civs and history is a boundary between what exists and what happens to it. Every downstream question the GATE asks inherits this. 'How thin can the physical substrate be' is a question about a part; 'does generation order equal simulation strata' is a question about whether the chart is even a chart. A regeneration contract phrased as 'deepening a lower stratum regenerates everything above' assumes the strata are stacked like cuts on a side of beef, but nothing regenerates a process the way it regenerates a population, and the one stage that consumes all the others sits at the top of the stack being treated as its final slice.
- **P1**: Nothing is allowed to cross a stratum line, and nobody noticed
  - When: Once civilizations are the unit that carries derived facets and Souls are their members, the chart has decided that everything belongs to exactly one civ. You can cut smaller afterwards and never bigger. The things that make a deep history feel deep are precisely the ones that cross: a religion held in three civs and heresy in a fourth, a language family older than any polity that speaks it, a diaspora that is a people without a state, a trade league, a species that seeded two civs and outlives both. The condition asks in GATE whether celestial bodies are Souls and whether the substrate can be thin, but never asks whether any entity is meant to live across a stratum boundary. The answer is being fixed by omission, in slice one, in the interfaces the OUTCOME says are designed for later deepening — and a later slice that wants a cross-civ institution will find that the only division available to it runs through the middle of one.
- **P1**: The whole animal is bought and three cuts are kept
  - When: The probe brings into existence a few hundred systems, their worlds, species stubs, civilizations, every member Soul of every civilization, and 120-plus centuries of events over all of it. The cost curve charges for exactly that: systems times civs times members times centuries. What the condition keeps is a chronicle, a set of cost numbers, a FINDINGS entry, a Mind-State mirror and some beads. Everything else is declared disposable — worlds are disposable until played. A butcher who pays for a whole carcass, takes three cuts and puts the rest in the bin has not made a cheap meal, he has made an expensive one and mispriced it, and he will keep believing the loin is affordable. The condition never states whether the generated world is itself an artifact worth keeping, whether a second run reuses any of it, or what it would even mean to keep one. Its affordability judgement is therefore being made about a body it intends to discard entirely, which is the one circumstance in which the cost of the body is the whole cost.
- **P1**: Parts with no one to receive them
  - When: In a division where each part is owed to a named recipient, a piece nobody is due is not a spare, it is an unfinished cut. This condition produces a chronicle for 'a human', a cost curve so that 'the next engine bottleneck is measured', species stubs for a Dixon stratum that has not been built, an event log for a queryability check, beads for strata deliberately stubbed, and a brainstorm doc for an implementation phase that no later bullet refers back to. Only two of these name who receives them, and neither names a person or a next step that has to act. The consequence is not rudeness but drift: the chronicle's readability has no reader to fail, the cost curve has no roadmap owner to accept or reject the figure, and the stubs have no consumer to test whether the interface admits what it must. A part with no recipient gets made to whatever standard the maker felt like.
- **P1**: The exclusion line is drawn at the appetite of an undecided part
  - When: OUT excludes terrain and biome detail 'beyond what species stubs consume'. The species stub is the same part OUTCOME calls scaffold-thin, that GATE asks whether to generate or stub behind its interface, and that the brainstorm is meant to rule on before implementation. So the cut line between what is in scope and what is out is positioned relative to a piece whose size will not be known until after the ruling that OUT was written to constrain. A generous ruling on Dixon-grade evolution silently widens the in-scope terrain work; a thin one silently narrows it; and in both cases the exclusion did no excluding at the moment it mattered. This is not an argument that OUT bans too much or too little — it is that the boundary is not at a fixed place on the body, so nobody can say, before the work starts, which side of it a given piece of terrain work is on.
- **P2**: Two charts over one body, reconciled by analogy
  - When: GATE asks whether stratified genesis settles the deferred time-model ruling — whether generation order is the same architecture as simulation strata. These are two different divisions of the same world: one cuts by order of construction, the other by rate of change. They coincide only if everything that is built early also changes slowly, and the condition's own material argues otherwise, since a galaxy scaffold is built first and never changes while a civ's facets are built late and change constantly. Proposing to settle one chart by adopting the other is the move that produces a set of parts belonging to neither cuisine: strata that are neither a build order anyone can regenerate against nor a pace layering anyone can simulate against.
- **P2**: A flat list of cuts with no order of breakdown
  - When: The DONE WHEN bullets are presented as an unordered set of conditions, but several of them foreclose the others. The brainstorm ruling fixes the stratum boundaries; the galaxy scaffold fixes what civ genesis is able to consume; the interfaces fix what any later slice can still ask a stubbed stratum for. A carcass has a strict order of breakdown for exactly this reason — the first cuts decide what cuts remain available — and a plan that lists them as parallel acceptance items invites them to be attempted in the order that is convenient on the day. This is a claim about sequence, not about which bullets matter more.
- **P2**: The chart has four parts and the criteria name seven
  - When: OUTCOME's chain names galaxy, species, civs-with-Souls and history. The acceptance criteria then require systems with spectral class, habitable worlds, homeworlds, neighborhoods, contact reach, member Souls, and dated era-scale events. Between them these are at least seven distinct parts collapsed into four named stages, and the collapse hides the joints where the ambiguity actually lives: whether habitability is a property of the system or of a world, whether a homeworld is a world or a relation between a civ and one, and whether a Soul is a portion of a civ or a part in its own right that a civ happens to contain. Each of those is a place where two later pieces of work will assume different cuts.
